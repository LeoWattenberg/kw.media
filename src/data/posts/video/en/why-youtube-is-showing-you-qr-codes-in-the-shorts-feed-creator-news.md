## AI-Driven Brand Matching and the Evolution of Creator Partnerships

YouTube is fundamentally restructuring how creators connect with sponsors. The platform is moving away from [Brand Connect](/youtube-tips-en/youtube-updates-brandconnect-expansion-and-new-streaming-features/) in favor of a more automated, AI-driven system now called YouTube Creator Partnership. This new framework is integrated directly into [Google Ads](/en/ads/) and YouTube Studio, signaling a shift toward a B2B model where the platform takes a more active role in matchmaking.

The core of this update is the integration of Gemini AI. Rather than brands manually searching for creators through traditional discovery methods, Google is leveraging AI to analyze data from over 3 million creators. This allows advertisers to input highly specific criteria—such as "tech creators with high Gen Z retention"—and have the AI automatically generate a list of matching channels.

For creators, this introduces a significant tension between data privacy and discoverability. YouTube is attracting brands by promising substantial performance gains, claiming 30% higher conversion rates and an 86% better return on ad spend (ROAS) through these AI matches. However, the "cost" of this visibility is transparency. If you choose to keep your detailed channel insights private within your Studio tab, you effectively make yourself invisible to this automated matching system. While relying on an AI to secure sponsorships may not be for everyone, those who wish to be discovered by brands via Google Ads will likely need to embrace this openness.

## Twitch and the Limits of "TikTok-ification"

While many platforms are currently racing to implement interactive, short-form elements to monetize live viewers, Twitch is providing a cautionary tale about blindly copying competitors. On March 31st, Twitch is completely removing its "Combos" feature.

The decision comes after significant pushback from the desktop gaming community. Combos utilized vertical stream animations designed for smartphone users; however, when these elements appeared on traditional 16:9 desktop streams, they were widely perceived as annoying spam rather than value-added engagement. This move suggests that Twitch has recognized a misalignment between TikTok's mobile-first design philosophy and the core viewing habits of its primary audience.

## Converting Passive Desktop Viewers into Active Mobile Creators

A curious new development has appeared in the [YouTube Shorts desktop feed](/youtube-tips-en/we-took-your-biggest-complaints-directly-to-youtube-live-creator-news/): giant QR codes appearing next to videos. While this may seem like an odd addition to a browser experience, it is a strategic attempt to bridge the gap between passive consumption and active creation.

When a user scans one of these QR codes, the process is seamless: it opens the YouTube app on their smartphone, launches the Shorts camera, and automatically loads the specific video track into remix mode. 

This feature addresses a specific friction point in the current ecosystem. Data across the kw.media portfolio indicates that approximately 20% of Shorts views now occur on connected TVs and desktop browsers. Despite this significant portion of traffic, all primary creation tools—including remix, collab, and green screen—remain exclusive to the mobile app. By placing QR codes directly in the desktop feed, YouTube is attempting to convert these passive viewers into active contributors by directing them immediately to the only place where they can actually create content.

## Adobe Project Moonlight: The AI Co-Worker in the Edit Suite

For those handling post-production, Adobe has introduced "Project Moonlight," an AI co-worker integrated into Premiere Pro and Photoshop. Powered by Firefly AI, this system is designed to learn a user's specific editing style over time.

The goal of Project Moonlight is to reduce the manual labor involved in navigating complex menus. Instead of manually executing every step, editors can use text commands to perform tasks such as rough cuts or color grading. Essentially, the AI acts as an assistant that understands the intent behind the edit, streamlining the workflow from a series of clicks to a conversation.

However, it is important for creators to evaluate whether these AI features justify the cost of an Adobe subscription. While the technology is impressive, there are powerful alternatives available depending on your needs. For those seeking high-end video editing without a monthly fee or a mandatory AI assistant, DaVinci Resolve remains a top recommendation. Similarly, for image editing, open-source options like Photopea provide excellent functionality without the ecosystem lock-in.

As AI continues to permeate every stage of the creator workflow—from how you are discovered by brands to how you cut your footage—the balance between automation and creative control becomes increasingly important. Whether it is Gemini matching you with a sponsor or Firefly handling your rough cuts, the tools are becoming more powerful, but the decision to use them remains with the Creator.
