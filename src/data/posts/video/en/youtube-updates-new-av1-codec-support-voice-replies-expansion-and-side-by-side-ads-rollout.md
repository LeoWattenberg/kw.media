## New YouTube Updates: AV1 Codec, Side-by-Side Ads, and Android Voice Replies

YouTube continues to refine both its technical infrastructure and its creator toolset. This week's updates range from high-end video encoding for [live streamers](/en/live/) to new [monetization formats](/en/creator/) and engagement tools for mobile users. Whether you are a technical power user or a creator focused on community growth, these changes impact how your content is delivered and how you interact with your audience.

### AV1 Codec Support for Live Streams

One of the most significant technical updates is the introduction of the AV1 codec for select live streams. While YouTube has not provided a strict definition of what constitutes "high-traffic" streams, this codec is now available for those who meet that unspecified threshold. 

For creators using software encoders like OBS, you can now switch your output to AV1. However, there is a caveat regarding the viewer's experience: while you can transmit AV1 data to YouTube's servers, it remains unclear exactly when and how that data is delivered to the end viewer. 

To verify if your stream is actually being served as AV1 to your audience, you can use the "Stats for Nerds" feature. By right-clicking on the video player during a live broadcast and selecting "Stats for Nerds," you can view the current codec in use. It is important to note a technical distinction here: AV1 and AV01 are not the same, so be sure to check the specific label in the stats window to confirm the codec's status.

### Expanding Side-by-Side Ads for Live Streams

The rollout of [side-by-side ads](/youtube-tips-en/youtube-updates-profanity-rule-changes-side-by-side-ads-and-livestream-community-leaderboard/) has now reached 100%. This feature is designed to be less intrusive than traditional mid-rolls, as it allows viewers to continue watching the live content while an advertisement plays alongside the video.

However, eligibility for [side-by-side ads](/youtube-tips-en/youtube-updates-mid-roll-ad-bug-and-editing-restrictions-incoming/) is not automatic; it requires a very specific configuration of your stream settings:

*   **Latency Settings:** Your stream must be set to "Normal Latency." This may be a hurdle for many creators who typically use "Low" or "Ultra-Low" latency to maintain real-time interaction with their chat.
*   **Captions:** All captions must be disabled. This includes both manually uploaded captions and YouTube's automatic captioning features.
*   **Ad Placement:** The ad automator must be configured to let YouTube decide the placement of ads automatically.

If you wish to take advantage of this non-interruptive ad format, you will need to adjust these settings in your [streaming dashboard](/en/youtube-tips/). Creators will have to weigh the benefit of side-by-side monetization against the loss of low-latency interaction and accessibility provided by captions.

### Voice Replies Expand to Android

After a six-month beta period on iOS, voice replies are now expanding to creators on Android. This feature allows for a more personal way to engage with fans by responding to comments with audio. 

Despite the expansion, there is a significant limitation regarding workflow: voice replies can only be recorded and posted via the main YouTube app. They are currently unavailable in YouTube Studio, whether on mobile or desktop. For professional creators who manage their community engagement through the dedicated Studio app—similar to how one might use Discord or WhatsApp for communication—this restriction makes the feature less convenient.

To prevent voice replies from becoming overly long or disruptive, YouTube has implemented a 30-second limit per reply. While this keeps responses concise, the requirement to switch to the main app remains a point of friction for those with established management workflows.

### Google Lens Integration for Shorts

Finally, YouTube is integrating Google Lens directly into the Shorts experience to improve viewer discovery. When a user pauses a Short, they will now see "oval chips" at the top of the screen. One of these is a new "Lens" chip featuring the Google Lens icon.

By clicking this chip, viewers can select a specific area of the paused frame. Google Lens then analyzes that area to provide information about the objects or text on screen. This removes the need for users to take a screenshot and manually upload it to a separate app to identify something they saw in a video. 

While this is a quality-of-life improvement for viewers, it represents YouTube's ongoing effort to bridge the gap between content consumption and real-world information retrieval through Google's ecosystem.
